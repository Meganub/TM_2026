1971

ARPAnet, the origin of the internet as a military tool.

In the year 1945, two things happened: World War 2 ended, and the term “Internet” was coined by the United States War Department. Although this term was originally meant to designate a network of radios, this term is now commonly used to refer to the World Wide Web, who didn’t come into existence until 1989 in the CERN (that’s us!).

The US department of Defense originally funded ARPANET, more bureaucratically known as the Advanced Research Projects Agency Network, served as a way to link files between different departments of the US government, and was the first network to implement the TCP/IP protocol suite. The first message sent over ARPANET was “LO”, which was meant to be “LOGIN”, but the system crashed after the first two letters were sent. The first successful message sent over ARPANET was “LOGIN”.

1989

Routing the internet through telephone lines.

The Dial-up Internet connection was the first commercially available way to connect to the internet back in the early 90s all the way up to the late 2000s. Nowadays, we use somthing called broadband or fiber-optic, dial-up has been mostly left behind.

The Way Dial-up worked and why it was ingenious is that it used the already existant telephone lines to connect regular people to the internet using audio. You'd have to buy a modem to use dial-up. The way it would work is that your computer would send data as audio through telephone lines, and a modem at some telecommunications company would demodulate that audio to reconstruct your data.

At first, as all things early internet were, only universities really had access to dial-up internet, and students could potentially use it from their own homes using university technology. The first commercially available form of dial-up was offered by Software Tool & Die, called "The World" in 1990. AT&T and Sprint would quickly hop on this bandwagon and start offering their own access to consumers.

1992

The early 90's internet can only be described as a sort of wild west. There weren't any real limitations or regulations on what one could do at that point and the sheer learnability of html and css made it so that anyone with access to the internet could go to GeoCities and code their own personal website.

Usually, people would use services like GeoCities to host their websites. These websites were normally just introductory pages and a sort of proof of existance to the world of the internet, where one only had a website to show that they were there and they did their best to have a voice in the endless abyss that is the internet.

One example i really like citing when considering the early 90's internet, is a small satirical website known as "The Church of Euthanasia", that you can still find at churchofeuthanasia.org. This website, launched in 1995, was a satire of the rise of religion-based websites on the 90's internet. The creators of this website, based in Boston, gained notoriety because of the sheer shock value this website created. This is very good way to show how even in the early days, shock and extreme emotions is the way to get yourself any clicks. You had to be loud and different to have any sort of attention.

1989

A file sharing service, and a pirate's dream.

Limewire, launched in 2000, was a peer-to-peer file sharing network. Peer-to-peer meaning that users would share files directly with each other, instead of using precious server space and bandwidth. This file sharing platform let users share music, video and images for free, if you had an internet connection. This got them into a lot of controversy, because piracy very soon became an issue for major distributors of music specifically, since any and all albums would be ripped and placed into Limewire.

1996

The precursor to image boards.

Before talking about image boards, it is important to introduce their predecessor: textboards. Originating from Japan, they are simple websites, where one could be fully anonymous and start threads, similarly to how modern forums operate. Although this technology never really left Japan, it gave rise to Imageboards, the first one being Ayashii World, launched by Shiba Masayuki in 1996. This gave rise to boards like Danbooru and the more influential Futaba Channel, colloquially known as ”2chan” in Japan, which, in 2003, inspired Christopher “Moot” Poole to make a very important board: 4Chan

2012

The first short-form video platform

Vine was founded in 2012 by three friends who met while working for another company: Dom Hofmann, Rus Yusupov, and Colin Kroll. It presented itself quite simply: an app that let you post looping, 6 second long videos for everyone to see. The appeal of such a website was quickly seen by Twitter Inc. and it bought out the whole website before it even launched, for a lump sum of $30 million. Vine, to put it simply, was hugely successful. 2016 YouTube was filled with Vine compilations, and whole media personalities made their debut on Vine. Recognizable names like the Paul brothers, Jack Doherty and Zach King. Vine’s UI decisions were quite genius. For example, competitors like Instagram and twitter had clunky tabs at the bottom, while Vine’s switcher was hidden on the home button at the top left. The concept that was quickly understood by the makers of Vine’s UI was that users like to stay on what they’re doing, and the tabs were just getting in the way. The six-second duration was not chosen through obscure research or psychology, it was chosen through trial and error. They had actually tried other durations like 4,5,7 and even 10 seconds, and arrived at the 6 second duration as a nice in-between. 10 seconds made file sizes too big, and 5 seconds felt too short. The looping feature was actually done on accident, as they were trying to make the cutoff at the end of those 6 seconds a bit less anticlimactic. This loop feature was actually what made vine what it is, because it pushed the comedy on the platform to integrate the loop, making it so that a joke has to be heard 2 or 3 times to be understood, farming loops and watch time. Due to the high supply of views, one would assume that vine would insert a built-in ad system, but, unlike the greedy corporate shareholders today, Vine’s execs thought that inserting pre or mid-roll ads onto 6 second videos would “absolutely destroy the user experience”. That meant that even if you had 100 million loops on a video, you made a crisp 0 dollars out of it. Because of this, users had to find other ways to monetize their creations, notably with product placement, where companies would pay anything in the range of 100$ to 40000$ per vine, depending on how many followers or how much reach users had. To conclude, the reason why vine is important to internet culture is because it was quite literally the centre of human attention on the internet up until its demise, posting numbers as high as 200 million daily active users in December of 2015.
